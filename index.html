Permit to Work

5 checks before you sign a Permit to Work

A permit is only as good as the checks behind it. Before signing, I walk to the work area and confirm five things.

  1. Scope matches reality. The work, location and time on the permit must match what the crew is actually about to do.
  2. Risk assessment is approved and briefed. The MSRA or JHA is current, and the workers can explain the main hazards in their own words.
  3. Isolation is verified. For electrical or mechanical work, check that LOTO is in place and tested, not just recorded on paper.
  4. People and equipment are competent. Operators, riggers and supervisors hold valid certificates, and tools carry current inspection tags.
  5. Emergency arrangements are ready. Fire extinguishers, a fire watch where needed, first aid and the nearest exit route are in place.

If any one of these fails, the permit waits. A few minutes of checking is far cheaper than an incident.

Training

Toolbox talks that workers actually listen to

A toolbox talk that feels like a lecture is forgotten before the crew reaches the work face. These habits make them count.

  1. Keep it under ten minutes. One topic, one message.
  2. Tie it to today's task. Talk about the lift, the excavation or the scaffold they are about to use, not a generic poster.
  3. Ask, don't tell. Ask "What could go wrong here?" and let the crew answer. People remember what they say themselves.
  4. Use real examples. Share a near miss from the site, with the lesson, and without blaming anyone.
  5. Follow up. Record attendance, then check on site that what was discussed is actually being done.

Done well, the toolbox talk becomes the moment the crew sets its own standard for the day.

Lifting Operations

Before the lift: the document check that prevents incidents

Most lifting problems can be spotted on paper before the crane moves. Before any lift, I review the following.

  1. The lifting plan. Load weight, radius, ground conditions and the planned position of the crane.
  2. Equipment certificates. Crane, slings, shackles and spreader beams all need valid inspection and third-party certificates.
  3. Operator and rigger competence. Licences and certificates must be valid and match the equipment in use.
  4. Communication. A trained signalman, an agreed signal method and a clear stop authority.
  5. Exclusion zone. Barriers and signage in place, with nobody under the load or inside the lift radius.

If a certificate has expired or a document is missing, the lift does not go ahead until it is fixed.
